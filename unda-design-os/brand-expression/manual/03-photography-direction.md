# 3. Photography Direction

Extends [Photography foundations](../../design-system/foundations/photography.md) and [Art direction](../../creative-direction/art-direction.md) with concrete direction for commissioning and selecting images.

## Subject matter

Real work, real people, real environments: designers reviewing work, engineers pairing on a problem, a travel consultant on a call, an office or co-working space actually in use. Never generic "business handshake" or "person pointing at a whiteboard laughing" stock photography.

## Lighting

Natural or naturalistic light — window light, overcast daylight, practical interior lighting. Avoid hard studio flash, dramatic rim lighting, or anything that reads as a commercial shoot rather than a documented moment. Low-to-moderate contrast, matching the calm register in [Creative vision](../../creative-direction/creative-vision.md).

## Color grading

Neutral white balance, natural skin tones, moderate saturation, soft contrast — consistent with the [neutral color foundation](../../design-system/foundations/color.md). No heavy filters, no orange-and-teal grading, no black-and-white treatments used as a stylistic default. A photo should look like it could have been taken yesterday, not "of a design era."

## Composition & framing

One clear subject per image. Tight, intentional cropping — a single subject or moment, not a wide shot included to "be safe" ([Art direction](../../creative-direction/art-direction.md)). Natural perspective; avoid dramatic wide-angle distortion or extreme low/high angles. Leave room for typography where an image will carry text (rule-of-thirds negative space, not centered subjects when text will overlay).

## Backgrounds

Flat, quiet, uncluttered. A busy background competes with the subject — prefer shallow depth of field or genuinely tidy real environments over staged clean sets.

## Diversity & authenticity

People shown should reflect who Unda actually serves and employs — a real range of background, gender, age, and ability, shown doing real work, not posed for the camera. Authenticity beats polish: a slightly imperfect real moment is more trustworthy than a perfectly lit fake one.

## Technology imagery

Real screens, real devices, real interfaces — actual product UI wherever possible, not placeholder mockups or generic "code on a screen" stock shots. When hardware is shown, show it in use, not as an isolated product-shot.

## Business imagery

Real meetings, real decision-making moments, real client environments (with permission) — never generic "corporate stock" imagery (empty conference rooms, exaggerated smiling handshakes).

## African innovation imagery

Per [Expression Principles §7](./01-expression-principles.md): show real African cities, offices, and professionals at the same quality and modernity bar as any global tech brand's imagery. Explicitly avoid the two failure modes international media defaults to — poverty/hardship framing and generic "safari landscape" framing when the subject is business or technology. The goal is recognition: someone from Lagos, Nairobi, or Accra should see their actual working world represented accurately, not exoticized or diminished.

## Good examples

- A designer sketching on paper next to a laptop showing the same concept mid-refinement.
- A real office space, natural light, people mid-conversation, not looking at camera.
- A close, tightly-cropped shot of hands typing, or a screen showing real work in progress.

## Bad examples

- Stock photography with visible watermarked-stock "tells" (exaggerated expressions, generic office plants, impossible lighting).
- Diverse-looking-but-clearly-staged "corporate diversity" stock sets.
- Poverty-framed or landscape-only imagery used to represent African business/technology.
- Heavy Instagram-style filters, HDR skies, or any grading that dates the image to a specific year.
